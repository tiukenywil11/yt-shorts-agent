# snooze-button

- Created: 2026-11-07
- Channel: Small Change Lab
- Pillar / series: Body / Habit Myths (myth -> what the research says -> real habit)
- Working title: "Is Hitting Snooze Really That Bad?"
- Topic: #10 "The no-snooze wake-up" in `creative/trending-habit-topics-2026-11.xlsx` (trend score 15.08), reframed as a myth check because the cited study tests the claim directly. Chosen for pillar balance: episodes 1-5 were Mind, Body, Mind, Money, Money; Body is at 1 of 5 against a ~20% target. Not a repeat of `02-night-wind-down` (bedtime screens); this is the morning side.
- Central idea: the common belief is that snoozing leaves you groggier. In a lab study of habitual snoozers, 30 minutes of snoozing improved or did not affect cognitive performance on rising versus an abrupt wake-up, for about 6 minutes of lost sleep. A planned, brief snooze window is fine; the real habit is deciding it ahead of time.
- Audience: students and early-career workers who snooze and feel guilty.
- Hook (myth hook, sentence 1 <=12 words): "Hitting snooze may not ruin your morning." (Batch log: outcome, claim, myth, claim, outcome, myth.)
- Payoff: three steps (set one alarm for the time you need to be up, if you snooze decide the window the night before, stand up when the last alarm rings), then "Try it today: decide your snooze window before bed."
- Visual idea: our own take, no trend footage. Inch beside a paper alarm clock and bed edge; coral MYTH stamp on "snooze = groggy", mint ACTUALLY stamp on the finding. Cite card, then three step slates. Body tint background, NOT MEDICAL ADVICE tag from the cite card on.
- Loop: last frame returns to the hook layout, clock now showing a planned window label.

## Source (verified 2026-11-07 via Crossref + OpenAlex abstract)
- Sundelin, T., Landry, S., & Axelsson, J. (2023/2024). Is snoozing losing? Why intermittent morning alarms are used and how they affect sleep, cognition, cortisol, and mood. *Journal of Sleep Research*. doi:10.1111/jsr.14054.
- Findings used (from the abstract): Study 2 was a within-subjects lab study with polysomnography on habitual snoozers (n = 31); 30 min of snoozing improved or did not affect cognitive test performance directly upon rising compared with abrupt awakening; about 6 min of sleep lost; no clear effects on cortisol awakening response, morning sleepiness, mood or overnight sleep architecture. Study 1 (n = 1732) found snoozing is widespread.
- Limit line for the card: `31 habitual snoozers`, `one lab study`, evidence strength varied (Bayes factors).
- Not claimed: that snoozing helps everyone; any effect beyond the first moments after waking; sleep-disorder, chronotype or health advice; that longer or repeated snoozing over weeks is harmless; any product or app. Year to show on card: confirm print year before stating (Crossref lists 2023 online); script says "a 2023 lab study".
